    Flow and gradient

    Most AS is easy: high gradient means severe. The hard cases are a small valve area with a low gradient. This pathway follows this patient's numbers; the highlighted boxes are where they sit.

    Low flow = stroke volume index <35 mL/m². CT calcium making severe AS likely: ≥2000 AU men, ≥1200 AU women (very likely ≥3000 / ≥1600; unlikely <1600 / <800).

    Why the numbers disagree: measurement pitfalls

    • LVOT diameter underestimated. The error is squared, so AVA and SV come out falsely small. The most common cause of “normal-flow low-gradient” AS.
    • Vmax not from the best window. Ask whether right parasternal / Pedoff imaging was done; a missed window underestimates the gradient.
    • Hypertension during the echo. High afterload lowers the gradient. Repeat once BP is controlled.
    • Atrial fibrillation. Values should be averaged over 5 or more beats.
    • Small body size. Use indexed AVA; a small woman with AVA 0.9 may only have moderate AS.
    • Pressure recovery in a small ascending aorta (<3 cm) makes Doppler overestimate severity.
    • Mitral regurgitation jet mistaken for the AS jet (MR starts earlier and lasts longer).

    High gradient but AVA >1.0: high-flow states

    Anemia, fever or sepsis, hyperthyroidism, AV fistula (dialysis), significant aortic regurgitation, pregnancy, and pain or anxiety on the day. Treat the cause and repeat the echo; if flow is normal and the gradient stays high, treat as severe.

    When to intervene

    Sources: 2020 ACC/AHA valvular heart disease guideline; 2025 ESC/EACTS valvular heart disease guideline. ✓ = met by the data entered · ? = needs a test or more information · – = not met.

    Moderate AS

    No routine intervention for isolated moderate AS outside trials. Consider AVR if the patient is already going for other cardiac surgery. Moderate AS with LV dysfunction and heart failure is an active trial question; discuss with cardiology rather than assume it is bystander disease.

    When intervention will not help

    Life expectancy under 1 year from other disease, or comorbidities (advanced dementia, severe frailty, end-stage lung disease) that would stop symptoms or quality of life improving after AVR. Here, focus on goals of care and symptom management. Balloon valvuloplasty is a temporary bridge or palliation in selected cases, with restenosis within months.

    What makes TAVI harder or not possible

    • Access: small, calcified or tortuous iliofemoral arteries (alternative access: subclavian, carotid, transcaval).
    • Annulus outside available valve sizes; heavy LVOT calcium (annular rupture risk).
    • Low coronary heights or narrow sinuses: coronary obstruction risk.
    • Bicuspid valve, especially in younger patients: favours SAVR.
    • Active endocarditis and LV thrombus: contraindications.
    • Other surgery needed anyway: multivessel CAD for CABG, aortic root or ascending aorta >4.5 cm, severe primary mitral or tricuspid disease.

    What favours TAVI over surgery

    • Age ≥70 (ESC) or >80 (ACC/AHA), frailty, reduced mobility.
    • Prior sternotomy or patent grafts, porcelain aorta, chest radiation, chest wall deformity.
    • Severe lung, liver or kidney disease that raises surgical risk.
    • Small annulus in an older patient (TAVI gives lower gradients than small surgical valves).

    Workup and referral

    Tick off what is done; the unchecked items go into the referral note.

    Island Health route

    Refer to cardiology, who bring the patient to the structural Heart Team (TAVI program at Royal Jubilee Hospital). Include the echo, symptoms with their impact, frailty and cognition, goals of care, comorbidities and the patient's wishes. Confirm the current local referral form and process before sending.

    Coronary disease before TAVI

    Coronary angiography or coronary CT before AVR. Significant proximal disease (≥90% in vessels ≥2.5 mm, or FFR ≤0.80) is reasonable to treat with PCI around the time of TAVI; multivessel disease suitable for CABG points toward SAVR + CABG.

    Inpatient care with severe AS

    Hemodynamics and medications

    • Preload dependent: avoid dehydration and over-diuresis. Diurese for congestion, but watch for falling BP and rising creatinine.
    • Vasodilators with care: avoid nitrates (sublingual or IV) and PDE5 inhibitors in severe AS; start ACE inhibitor or ARB low and titrate. Treat hypertension: it is common and adds afterload.
    • Hypotension: fluids if dry; phenylephrine or norepinephrine to keep coronary perfusion. Avoid deep sedation that drops SVR.
    • Atrial fibrillation: poorly tolerated because atrial kick is lost. Aim for sinus rhythm or tight rate control; urgent cardioversion if unstable.
    • Statins do not slow AS; use them for their usual indications only.

    Decompensated severe AS

    • Cautious diuresis, oxygen, treat triggers (AF, infection, anemia, ischemia).
    • Involve cardiology early: urgent TAVI (or balloon valvuloplasty as a bridge) is an option in cardiogenic shock or refractory heart failure.
    • Low-output state with LVEF reduced: ICU with invasive monitoring.

    Non-cardiac surgery, including hip fracture

    • Urgent surgery such as hip fracture should not be delayed for AVR. Tell anesthesia early; they choose the technique, often with an arterial line and careful neuraxial or general anesthesia to avoid sudden falls in SVR.
    • Targets: sinus rhythm, normal volume, keep SVR and BP up, avoid tachycardia, early vasopressor for hypotension, careful post-op fluids.
    • Elective higher-risk surgery with symptomatic severe AS: AVR first. Asymptomatic severe AS: elective surgery is reasonable with monitoring.
    • Post-op: watch for hypotension, AF, demand ischemia, delirium and heart failure.

    GI bleeding: Heyde syndrome

    Severe AS shears von Willebrand multimers (acquired von Willebrand type 2A) and is linked to bleeding GI angiodysplasia. Think of it with recurrent obscure GI bleeding and iron deficiency. von Willebrand multimer analysis supports it; valve replacement usually stops the bleeding.

    After TAVI: what to watch for on the ward

    • Conduction block: new LBBB or complete heart block, highest risk in the first 48 h but can appear up to 30 days. Pre-existing RBBB raises the risk. Daily ECG; ambulatory monitor if new LBBB or PR prolongation.
    • Access site: hematoma, pseudoaneurysm, retroperitoneal bleed (falling Hb, back or flank pain, hypotension).
    • Stroke (most in the first days), AKI from contrast, paravalvular leak, and later valve thrombosis or endocarditis.

    Antithrombotics after TAVI

    • No other indication for anticoagulation: aspirin alone (single antiplatelet) long term; routine dual antiplatelet adds bleeding.
    • Already on anticoagulation (for example AF): continue the anticoagulant alone, without adding aspirin or clopidogrel unless a recent stent needs it.
    • Do not start a DOAC just for the TAVI valve.
    • Recent PCI: follow the cardiologist's plan for antiplatelet duration.

    Endocarditis prophylaxis

    A TAVI or surgical prosthetic valve is a high-risk condition: antibiotic prophylaxis before dental work that manipulates the gums. Keep dental care up to date before elective AVR.
