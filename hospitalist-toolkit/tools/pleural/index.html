Imaging: what to look for

Chest X-ray

  • Blunting of the costophrenic angle needs about 200-300 mL on a PA film (about 50 mL on a lateral).
  • Meniscus sign; a large effusion pushes the mediastinum away. A white-out with shift toward it means collapse (endobronchial obstruction); no shift with a big effusion suggests a fixed mediastinum (mesothelioma, nodes) or trapped lung.
  • Supine film: hazy hemithorax, apical cap. Subpulmonic effusion: high flat "diaphragm", lateral peak.
  • Air-fluid level: hydropneumothorax (after a tap, bronchopleural fistula, esophageal rupture).

Ultrasound (use for every procedure)

  • More sensitive than CXR (detects 20 mL), shows depth, loculations and the safe site.
  • Anechoic fluid can be a transudate or exudate. Complex septated, echogenic or debris = exudate (septations: parapneumonic, TB, malignancy).
  • Pleural nodules or diaphragmatic thickening over 1 cm, liver metastases: malignancy.
  • Lung sliding after drainage; "jellyfish" atelectatic lung floating in fluid.

CT chest with contrast (pleural / late arterial phase)

  • Do it before draining to dryness when malignancy is possible (pleura is better seen with fluid present).
  • Malignancy: nodular, circumferential or mediastinal pleural thickening, parietal thickening over 1 cm.
  • Empyema: split pleura sign (enhancing parietal and visceral pleura), lenticular shape, gas without a procedure; look for lung abscess.
  • Also shows the cause: PE (CTPA), tumour, nodes, consolidation, ascites, chest tube position.

When to re-image

  • Pleural infection not settling at 24-48 h after drainage: ultrasound/CT for a residual collection.
  • After chest tube insertion: CXR for position. Before removal: CXR or ultrasound if unsure the space is drained.
  • 6-8 weeks after discharge for parapneumonic or unexplained effusion.

Chest tube orders and daily care
Suction or water sealwater seal is the default

Default: water seal (gravity drainage) for most chest tubes. Suction is used selectively. Routine suction does not shorten air leak, drain duration or length of stay after lung resection, and may prolong some air leaks.

Terms: every modern drainage unit has a water seal chamber that acts as a one-way valve. "Placing on water seal" just means taking the tube off active suction (usually set at −20 cmH₂O).

Put the tube on suction (or back on suction) if

  • The lung is not fully expanded, or the pneumothorax is enlarging on CXR while on water seal
  • Subcutaneous emphysema is increasing
  • Clinical deterioration: hypoxemia or hemodynamic compromise
  • Moderate-to-severe restrictive lung disease
  • Large air leak or suspected bronchopleural fistula

By situation

After lung resection (lobectomy, VATS)

  • STS consensus favours early change to water seal. In one RCT (n=254), water seal on POD1 once the lung was fully expanded shortened time to drain removal and lowered prolonged air leak (5.6% vs 0.7%).
  • Meta-analyses: no outcome benefit from suction, though a residual pleural space is more common on water seal.
  • Small air leaks usually seal on water seal; large leaks do not seem to benefit from it.
  • Hybrid option: moderate suction overnight, water seal during the day to allow mobilization.
  • Digital systems have no true water seal mode: with a leak they apply regulated suction even at low settings, so the analog-system data do not transfer directly.

Primary spontaneous pneumothorax

  • Do not start suction straight away: up to 70% of lungs re-expand by day 3 on drainage alone.
  • Add high-volume, low-pressure suction if the lung does not re-expand or the air leak persists (very low certainty evidence).
  • Do not let prolonged suction delay referral for definitive (surgical) management: that delay is linked to worse outcomes.

Traumatic pneumothorax / hemothorax

  • Less settled. A 2026 meta-analysis (5 RCTs, n=404): suction shortened tube duration by about 3 days and improved full lung expansion, but every trial was at high risk of bias.
  • 2026 SEAL IT cluster-randomized study (pneumothorax; excluded hemothorax over 300 mL and tubes placed before CT): initial water seal shortened tube duration (41 vs 51 h) with no increase in complications.

On positive-pressure ventilation

  • Keeping the tube on suction is reasonable: risk of tension physiology, and positive pressure can enlarge an air leak.
  • Help the leak heal: lower tidal volume, PEEP and inspiratory time, and liberate from the ventilator early.

Bubbling: when to worryair leak patterns

Expected: cough-only or intermittent bubbling that decreases over time is a healing alveolar-pleural leak. Concerning: continuous, new or worsening, lasting beyond 5 days, with clinical deterioration, or stopping suddenly while the patient gets worse. Then think system leak, bronchopleural fistula (BPF) or blocked tube, not a simple parenchymal leak.

Concerning patterns

  • Continuous bubbling through the whole respiratory cycle. Rule out a system leak first (loose connections, a side hole outside the chest wall, a damaged unit), then suspect a BPF: it bubbles continuously regardless of breathing phase and gets worse with positive pressure.
  • New bubbling, or worse after it had been decreasing: new injury, BPF, tube migration or a system leak.
  • Leak lasting more than 5 days: meets the definition of prolonged air leak; strongly suspect BPF. A persistent leak can be the only sign of a BPF.
  • Timing: BPF peaks 8-12 days after surgery, most common after pneumonectomy (especially right-sided). Other risks: diabetes, COPD, neoadjuvant therapy, prolonged mechanical ventilation.
  • Large leak on positive-pressure ventilation: minimize tidal volume, PEEP and inspiratory time.

Red flags: evaluate now

  • Bubbling with sudden dyspnea, chest pain, hemoptysis, hemodynamic instability, or new/increasing subcutaneous emphysema
  • Fever, purulent drainage or purulent cough: BPF with empyema
  • CXR: new or enlarging pneumothorax; a change in the air-fluid level (e.g. a falling level in a post-pneumonectomy space); air that reaccumulates quickly after clamping or tube removal
  • Bubbling that stops suddenly while subcutaneous emphysema increases or hypoxemia worsens: clotted, kinked or dislodged tube with possible tension physiology, not a healed leak

Next steps when bubbling is concerning

  1. Check the system: connections, tubing, insertion site, side-hole position on CXR, and clamp test along the tubing (briefly, at the bedside, to find where the leak is).
  2. CXR; CT if the source is unclear.
  3. Bronchoscopy for continuous leaks or suspected BPF, plus urgent thoracic surgery. A BPF almost always needs urgent intervention, often reoperation for a stump leak.
  4. Send pleural fluid if infection is a concern.
  5. STS: exclude both a BPF and a system leak before reoperation for prolonged air leak.

Usually reassuring

  • Bubbling only with cough or forced expiration, especially if less each day
  • Small leaks in the first few postoperative days: most seal within 5 days
  • Tidaling of the water column without bubbling
