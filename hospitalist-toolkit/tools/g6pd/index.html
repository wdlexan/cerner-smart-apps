Recognizing acute hemolysis

Timing: 24–72 h after exposure; Hb nadir ~7–10 days.

Clinical: fatigue, dyspnea, tachycardia, jaundice, dark/cola urine, back or abdominal pain, fever.

  • ↓ Hb; ↑ reticulocytes (lag 3–5 days)
  • ↑ indirect bilirubin, ↑ LDH, ↓ haptoglobin
  • UA blood positive with few RBCs (hemoglobinuria)
  • DAT negative
  • Smear: bite and blister cells; Heinz bodies
  • Creatinine and K⁺ (pigment AKI)
  • Low SpO₂ / cyanosis → co-oximetry methemoglobin

G6PD reference

Key facts

  • X-linked enzyme deficiency: RBCs cannot regenerate NADPH, so oxidant stress (drugs, infection, fava beans) causes acute hemolytic anemia.
  • Most common in African, Mediterranean, Middle Eastern, South and Southeast Asian ancestry.
  • Males are deficient or normal. Heterozygous females are variable (X-inactivation); a normal qualitative screen does not exclude risk, so use a quantitative assay.
  • Severity depends on variant: African A– usually mild, self-limited; Mediterranean variants can cause severe hemolysis.
  • Infection is the most common in-hospital trigger.

Testing pitfalls

  • False-normal during or right after hemolysis (young RBCs and reticulocytes have more enzyme). Repeat ~3 months later.
  • Recent transfusion masks deficiency.
  • Test before rasburicase, pegloticase, primaquine, tafenoquine or dapsone when time allows; tafenoquine needs a documented quantitative result.
  • Once confirmed, enter as a structured Cerner health issue / alert so pharmacy checking fires on future orders.

Low-to-no risk at usual doses (CPIC)

Usable with standard precautions even though many labels still warn:

  • Antibiotics: sulfamethoxazole (TMP-SMX), sulfadiazine, sulfisoxazole, ciprofloxacin, ofloxacin, norfloxacin, nalidixic acid, chloramphenicol
  • Antimalarial / rheum: chloroquine, hydroxychloroquine, quinine, sulfasalazine
  • Analgesic: aspirin ≤ 1 g/day
  • Diabetes: glyburide, tolbutamide
  • Other: vitamin K, vitamin C (usual doses), phenazopyridine, dimercaprol, doxorubicin, 4-aminosalicylic acid, mafenide

No CPIC recommendation (insufficient evidence): aspirin > 1 g/day, gliclazide, glipizide, glimepiride, chlorpropamide, tolazamide, moxifloxacin, mesalamine, probenecid, sodium nitrite, dabrafenib, trametinib, nicorandil, sulfacetamide, nitrofural, mepacrine.

Caution: megadose IV vitamin C has case reports of hemolysis and AKI.

Non-drug triggers

  • Infection (pneumonia, hepatitis, CMV, typhoid, COVID-19)
  • Fava beans
  • Naphthalene (mothballs)
  • DKA, severe acidosis
  • Some herbal products (e.g. Coptis/huanglian); henna in infants

Management

  1. Stop the offending drug; treat any precipitating infection.
  2. IV fluids for hemoglobinuria; monitor creatinine and K⁺.
  3. Daily CBC, retic, bilirubin, LDH until Hb stable.
  4. Transfuse for symptomatic or severe anemia or brisk ongoing hemolysis.
  5. Hematology consult for severe hemolysis, non-A– variant or chronic hemolysis.
  6. Record as a structured Cerner health issue / alert; note the trigger in the discharge summary.

Pearls

  • Significant methemoglobinemia: SpO₂ plateaus ~85% regardless of true saturation; look for an SpO₂–PaO₂ gap.
  • A– variant hemolysis is usually self-limited, but stop the drug anyway; Mediterranean variants can drop Hb 4–5 g/dL.
  • Treat a heterozygous female with an intermediate quantitative result as deficient for high-risk drugs.
