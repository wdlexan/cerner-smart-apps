Signs & symptoms

Often silent until bulky. Many patients present first with ascites, early satiety, or a bowel obstruction. Up to a third of new cases have no known primary at presentation.

Symptoms

  • Abdominal distension, bloating, increasing girth / clothes tight
  • Early satiety, anorexia, nausea, vomiting
  • Vague diffuse abdominal pain or discomfort
  • Change in bowel habit, constipation, intermittent colicky pain (partial obstruction)
  • Weight loss, fatigue, cachexia (may be masked by ascites weight gain)
  • Dyspnea – tense ascites splinting the diaphragm, or pleural effusion

Exam

  • Shifting dullness / fluid wave (ascites)
  • Palpable "omental cake" – firm upper/mid-abdominal mass
  • Sister Mary Joseph nodule (umbilical)
  • Blumer shelf – rectal shelf on DRE (pouch of Douglas deposits)
  • Left supraclavicular node (Virchow), cachexia, edema
  • Distension, tinkling or absent bowel sounds (obstruction)
  • Ovarian/adnexal mass (primary ovarian, or Krukenberg from gastric/colorectal)

  • What to send: colorectal → CEA, CA 19-9 · gastric → CEA, CA 19-9 (± CA 72-4) · ovarian → CA-125 (+ CEA/CA 19-9 to exclude GI, ± HE4) · unknown primary → CEA, CA 19-9, CA-125 (+ CA 15-3/HE4 in women; AFP, β-hCG, LDH if under 50).
  • Timing: baseline before chemo/surgery; then every cycle or every ~3 months; a rise in two consecutive values (same lab) is more meaningful than one.
  • Not useful for screening or for diagnosis without tissue; a normal marker does not exclude carcinomatosis (esp. mucinous or signet-ring tumours).
Ascitic fluid
  • Cell count + diff, albumin (SAAG), total protein, glucose, LDH, Gram stain/culture, cytology (send a large volume – ≥50–100 mL, more improves yield).
  • Peritoneal carcinomatosis pattern: SAAG <11 g/L, protein >25 g/L, high LDH, low glucose. Cytology is positive in most true carcinomatosis; negative cytology with SAAG ≥11 suggests liver metastases / portal hypertension instead.
Tissue diagnosis – needed before oncology can treat
  • Image-guided core biopsy of omentum/peritoneal nodule (IR) – usually best first route; cores allow IHC and molecular tests.
  • Diagnostic laparoscopy – gold standard for PCI and resectability; done by surgical oncology / gyne-onc, not a ward procedure.
  • Endoscopy/colonoscopy to find GI primary; mammogram if lobular breast suspected; gyne exam + pelvic US/MRI if ovarian.
  • IHC hints: CK7−/CK20+/CDX2+/SATB2+ → colorectal; CK7+/PAX8+/WT1+ → Müllerian (ovarian/tubal/primary peritoneal); CK7+/CK20± /CDX2± → gastric/upper GI; GATA3+ → breast; calretinin/WT1/D2-40 → mesothelioma.

Approach by primary tumour

General rule: tissue diagnosis + biomarkers + performance status + PCI decide everything. Early referral to medical oncology and, if possibly resectable, a peritoneal surface malignancy (CRS/HIPEC) centre or gyne-oncology.
Colorectal
  • ~5–10% have peritoneal metastases at diagnosis; peritoneal disease responds worse to chemo and has shorter survival than liver/lung-only metastases.
  • Workup: CT C/A/P, colonoscopy, CEA, MMR/MSI, RAS/BRAF, HER2. MRI-DWI ± diagnostic laparoscopy if CRS being considered.
  • Possible CRS candidate: ECOG 0–1, limited PCI (generally ≤15–20; lower is better), no unresectable extra-abdominal disease, achievable CC-0. Perioperative systemic chemo around CRS.
  • HIPEC: PRODIGE 7 – adding 30-min oxaliplatin HIPEC to complete CRS did not improve survival and added late complications. CRS itself is the key step; HIPEC use varies by centre (mitomycin C regimens still used).
  • Not a surgical candidate: systemic therapy by biomarkers (see Chemo); dMMR/MSI-H → immunotherapy first line.
  • Watch for: obstruction (often colonic – consider stent), ureteric obstruction, mucinous ascites.
Gastric / GEJ
  • Peritoneum is the commonest site of gastric spread; positive peritoneal cytology alone = M1 (stage IV). Worst prognosis of the three.
  • Workup: CT C/A/P, OGD + biopsy, HER2, PD-L1 CPS, MMR/MSI, Claudin 18.2. Staging laparoscopy with washings for cT3–4 or node-positive disease before curative-intent treatment – CT misses much peritoneal disease.
  • Treatment is mainly systemic. CRS/HIPEC only for very limited disease (PCI ≤6–7 in most series) or cytology-only disease after good chemo response, ideally in a trial (e.g. GASTRIPEC, PERISCOPE II). PIPAC is investigational.
  • Watch for: gastric outlet obstruction (duodenal stent or venting gastrostomy), bleeding from primary, linitis plastica → poor gastric emptying, Krukenberg tumours, malnutrition.
Ovarian / tubal / primary peritoneal (high-grade serous)
  • Most present at stage III–IV with peritoneal disease – this is still potentially curative-intent treatment, unlike GI primaries. Refer to gyne-oncology urgently.
  • Workup: CT C/A/P, CA-125 (and CEA/CA 19-9 to exclude GI primary), pelvic US/MRI, biopsy (core or laparoscopy), germline BRCA testing for all, tumour HRD.
  • Surgery goal = no residual disease. Primary debulking if complete resection is feasible and patient fit; otherwise neoadjuvant carboplatin/paclitaxel ×3 → interval debulking → 3 more cycles.
  • HIPEC: OVHIPEC-1 – cisplatin HIPEC at interval debulking improved median OS (≈34 → 46 months). Used at some centres.
  • Maintenance: PARP inhibitor (olaparib for BRCA-mutated; niraparib; olaparib + bevacizumab if HRD+).
  • Watch for: MBO at recurrence (common cause of death), recurrent ascites, pleural effusions, VTE.
Other primaries
  • Appendiceal / pseudomyxoma peritonei: slow-growing mucinous disease; CRS + HIPEC (mitomycin C) at an expert centre, even with high PCI. Long survival possible. Chemo responds poorly in low-grade disease.
  • Peritoneal mesothelioma: CRS + HIPEC in selected; otherwise systemic therapy. Ask about asbestos.
  • Pancreatic: very poor prognosis; systemic chemo (FOLFIRINOX or gemcitabine/nab-paclitaxel) if fit; early palliative care.
  • Lobular breast: can spread to peritoneum/GI wall and mimic GI primary; ER-positive often → endocrine-based therapy.
  • Unknown primary: core biopsy with IHC panel; women with Müllerian IHC profile are treated as ovarian.

Malignant bowel obstruction (MBO)

What to watch out for
  • Often partial, multi-level and intermittent; may settle and recur. Small bowel more common than colon in carcinomatosis.
  • Exclude mimics: constipation/faecal impaction, opioid-induced ileus, hypokalemia, hypomagnesemia, hypercalcemia, adhesions, volvulus, hernia, chemo-related (vinca, anticholinergics).
  • Red flags → urgent surgery: peritonitis, free air, closed-loop obstruction, rising lactate, fever with tachycardia, strangulation.
  • High-output vomiting → dehydration, AKI, hypokalemia, metabolic alkalosis; aspiration risk.
  • Missed: delirium from opioids + dehydration; pressure injuries; refeeding when feeding restarted.

Nutrition & goals of care
  • TPN: consider only if expected survival is months (not weeks), good performance status, and a plan (e.g. bridge to chemo or surgery). Discuss with oncology/palliative.
  • Median survival after MBO in advanced cancer is roughly 1–3 months; inoperable MBO often less. Have a goals-of-care conversation early and document it.
  • Involve palliative care early; many patients can go home with SC octreotide/antiemetics, venting PEG, or hospice.

Malignant ascites

Diagnose
  • Diagnostic paracentesis: cell count, albumin (SAAG), protein, glucose, LDH, culture, cytology (large volume).
  • SAAG <11 g/L → peritoneal carcinomatosis (diuretics rarely help). SAAG ≥11 g/L → massive liver mets, portal vein thrombosis, or coexisting cirrhosis (diuretics may help).
  • Rule out SBP if fever, pain or delirium (PMN ≥250/µL), and chylous ascites (milky; triglycerides) from lymphatic obstruction.

Chemo while obstructed or with ascites
  • Chemo-naive or chemo-sensitive tumours (ovarian, MSI-H colorectal, newly diagnosed colorectal) can open a partial MBO – worth an urgent oncology opinion.
  • After multiple lines and poor PS, chemo rarely reverses MBO; focus on comfort.
  • Bevacizumab: perforation and wound-healing risk – hold around surgery/stents, caution with active obstruction.
  • Oral agents (capecitabine, PARPi, TKIs) need a working gut – check absorption if vomiting.

Patient factors (estimate)

Going home: cancer in the lining of the abdomen

Your cancer has spread to the lining of your abdomen (the peritoneum). This can cause fluid build-up (ascites) and can slow or block the bowel. Your care team: ________________________

Things you can do

  • Eat small, frequent, soft or liquid meals. Avoid tough skins, nuts, seeds, raw vegetables and stringy meats if you have had a blockage.
  • Drink fluids through the day unless told otherwise.
  • Keep a record of bowel movements, gas, vomiting and your waist size or weight.
  • Take your bowel and anti-nausea medicines as prescribed. Do not stop pain medicines suddenly.

Signs your bowel may be blocking

  • Cramping belly pain that comes and goes
  • Swollen, hard belly
  • Vomiting, especially large amounts or dark/brown vomit
  • No stool and no gas for 24 hours or more

If this starts: stop solid food, sip clear fluids only, and call your team or go to the Emergency Department.

Your drain

  • Drain as instructed (usually not more than the amount your team set each time).
  • Keep the site clean and dry; change the dressing as shown.
  • Call your home care nurse or team if the drain stops working or leaks.

Call your team or go to the Emergency Department if

  • Vomiting that will not stop, or you cannot keep fluids down for 12–24 hours
  • No stool or gas for more than 24 hours with belly pain or swelling
  • Fever 38.0 °C or higher – especially if you are on chemotherapy (this can be an emergency)
  • Belly swelling getting worse quickly, or harder to breathe because of it
  • Drain site red, hot, swollen or leaking pus, or cloudy drain fluid
  • Much less urine than normal, very dizzy when standing, or very dry mouth
  • Leg swelling or pain in one leg
  • Yellow skin or eyes

Call 911 if

  • Sudden, severe belly pain, or a belly that is rigid and very tender
  • Vomiting blood or material that looks like coffee grounds; black or bloody stool in large amounts
  • Chest pain, sudden shortness of breath, or coughing blood
  • Fainting, confusion, or very hard to wake up
